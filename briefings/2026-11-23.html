1. A court throws out the "you didn't enforce your own anti-steering rules" case — for good

Order Nov 16, HousingWire Nov 17 (trade coverage of a federal court order; catch-up — missed by briefings #35–#38) | https://www.housingwire.com/articles/judge-dismisses-zea-antitrust/

  • What it is. Jorge Zea, a Florida broker who runs SnapFlatFee.com (sellers pay one flat fee to get on the MLS — the shared listing database — and handle showings and negotiations themselves), sued the National Association of Realtors (NAR) and 13 Florida Realtor associations and MLSs in August 2025. His claim was unusual: he did not attack NAR's rules, he said the groups agreed not to enforce them — specifically the rules against steering (a buyer's agent pointing clients toward or away from homes based on the pay offered), against filtering listings by compensation or brokerage, the written buyer-agreement requirement, and the rule that listing pages must show the listing broker's contact. On Nov 16, U.S. District Judge William P. Dimitrouleas (Southern District of Florida) dismissed the case with prejudice — Zea cannot refile these claims against these defendants — adopting the magistrate judge's October recommendation. The reasoning, in plain terms: sitting in the same governance bodies and making enforcement decisions together does not by itself prove a conspiracy; the complaint named no specific communications, votes, or directives showing an agreement to look the other way; and not enforcing a rule can just as plausibly be separate, independent choices. The court did not find that steering never happens. NAR's response restated its position: steering is banned under its policy and ethics code, and enforcement is handled by local associations and each MLS.
  • What it means for product opportunity. This closes one legal road for discount and flat-fee brokers: a court will not force enforcement on a pattern claim alone — what was missing was specific, dated evidence. That gap is a product surface (see Whitespace). It also sharpens Topic 14's standing post-settlement question: the commission rules changed on paper in 2024, but this ruling says the enforcement layer stays local and case-by-case, so tools that work inside that reality (documentation, disclosure, receipts) have firmer ground than tools that assume the rules enforce themselves.

Whitespace

The steering-evidence log. Zea's case failed on evidence, not on the underlying complaint — and the law-firm write-ups of the ruling say the same thing in so many words: discount brokers who believe they are being steered against should document it carefully now, because documentation is what a future case or complaint would need. Nobody in this record sells that documentation as a tool. The build is small and needs no MLS license, because it runs on the broker's own records: for each of the broker's listings, log the compensation offered, every showing request and its outcome, agent feedback, and any buyer-side comment about pay — timestamped as it happens, not reconstructed later — and export it as a clean complaint packet sized for the two bodies NAR itself names as the enforcers: the local association and the local MLS. It pairs with yesterday's exposure receipt (#38): both are paper-trail products for the cooperation fight, one for the seller conversation, one for the enforcement conversation. Honest limit: a log proves a pattern of events, not anyone's motive — the same distinction the court just drew — so the tool should present dated facts and let the broker draw conclusions.
