1. Arizona pulls its deed-fraud alert rule after agents and title firms sued — the alert system itself stays, voluntary

Nov 25, Inman (read in full) | https://www.inman.com/2026/11/25/arizona-deed-fraud-alert-policy-realtors-lawsuit/

  • What it is. The Arizona Department of Real Estate agreed to withdraw Substantive Policy Statement 2026.02, effective Nov 25. The policy, in force from Nov 1, told agents to tell clients about the state's Early Real Estate Sales Alert System (ERAS — a system that alerts a property owner when a pending sale involving their property is reported to the state) and to document the client's decision about taking part. The Arizona Association of Realtors and the Land Title Association of Arizona sued on Nov 18, saying the department had imposed a new requirement without the formal rulemaking process, with only a few days' notice, and pointing to possible penalties. Under the settlement, the department must remove the policy from its website and publish notice of the withdrawal. ERAS itself remains live and voluntary. Inman notes the settlement had not yet appeared in the Maricopa County Superior Court records it checked on Wednesday.
  • The catch in the system itself. The title association's Nov 3 security advisory, shared with Inman, said early testing showed ERAS sent information about a pending deal — address, parcel number, escrow opening date, and the title company handling it — to someone who did not own the property and whose right to receive it had not been checked. It warned that could help criminals spot live deals and attempt wire fraud, while saying it had not established an actual loss from an ERAS notice. Arizona Realtors also made the simpler point: a fraudster impersonating an owner has little reason to authorise a notice that could expose the fraud.
  • What it means for product opportunity. Fraud protection is a live category in this record (Property Shield's ownership checks for SmartMLS and Big Sky Country MLS, briefing #27), but Arizona just showed the limit of the mandate route: a state tried to make the alert a required conversation, the industry sued on process, and the rule lasted 24 days. Build angle: the sellable version is voluntary and verified — an ownership check that proves the person receiving an alert is the owner, sold to MLSs and title firms as a tool they offer, not a form agents must push. The ERAS leak point is the reverse-engineer lesson: an alert system that shares deal details before checking who is asking creates the very risk it claims to prevent.

2. Cotality puts its RiTA assistant inside another CRM — this time Zenu, in Australia — and it replies to past clients on its own

Nov 26, Australian Broker / Broker News AU (read in full; published Nov 25 23:16 UTC, which is Nov 25 PT) | https://www.brokernews.com.au/news/breaking-news/cotality-links-rita-assistant-to-zenu-real-estate-database-platform-290386.aspx

  • What it is. Cotality's RiTA AI assistant is now available inside Zenu, a CRM (customer relationship system — the software agencies use to track contacts and follow-ups) used by real estate agencies in Australia. The tie-up is live for all Zenu users. It joins Zenu's contact records with Cotality's property data so an agency can check how clean its database is and decide which contacts to call, email, or text first. Agencies can run automated two-way text exchanges with former clients, with RiTA replying on the agent's behalf. Contact changes, engagement history, and conversation outcomes pass back into Zenu automatically, and agents can attach a Cotality suburb or property report, generated on demand, to a text. This is Australia, not the US — filed as a shape from the same data company that runs Trestle in the US, not as a US competitor.
  • What it means for product opportunity. The pattern from yesterday's Rhea (#41) repeats, from the data-owner side: Cotality does not sell RiTA as a separate app agents must open; it embeds the assistant inside the CRM agents already live in, and its data is the reason it can rank who to contact. Contrast with the US approval-first tools in #40–#41 (Finmo, DataWeave): RiTA replies on its own. Partner angle for Jessica's world: the US version of this seat is already partly taken by Cotality itself through Trestle — any new agent product that wants Cotality property data inside a CRM is negotiating with a company that also sells the assistant that sits there. The open part is still the governed listing layer underneath, not another outreach bot on top.

3. A UAE down-payment platform raises $20 million — money for helping buyers cover the deposit, not for agent tools

Nov 26, FinTech Futures (single trade source; funding terms as reported there) | https://www.fintechfutures.com/venture-capital-funding/uae-proptech-sooner-lands-20m-seed-round

  • What it is. Dubai-based Sooner said it has raised $20 million in seed funding: $5 million in equity led by Nuwa Capital, with Annex Investments, 500 Global, Plus VC, and others taking part, plus a $15 million Shariah-compliant debt facility from Amwal Capital Partners. Sooner calls itself the UAE's first down-payment support platform — a buyer pays 10% down instead of the usual 20%, and the rest is split over up to 60 monthly payments. It says it left beta in July, has had about 9,000 applications, and has helped with roughly $10 million in home purchases so far — those last figures are the company's own, not verified here. It plans a home-equity credit product in 2027.
  • What it means for product opportunity. Mostly a capital signal, and a familiar one for this record: the big proptech money keeps going to companies that carry money (deposits, lending, equity) rather than to tools agents use. That matches the CRETI October picture in SOURCES.md — $1.65B across 34 rounds, with none of the biggest deals going to agent tools. No build or partner angle for JJ and Jessica here; filed so the funding picture stays honest on a quiet day.

Whitespace

The listing-marketing consent packet — one signed record of how each listing may be shown. Two stories this week point at the same missing paperwork. In New York (see Permission watch), a seller who wants a listing kept private must now sign a state disclosure form, and the agent must keep that form for at least three years. In Arizona, even after the mandate was withdrawn, Arizona Realtors told agents who still discuss ERAS to document the client's decision and keep it in the transaction file. So in both states the safe move is the same: a per-listing record, signed at listing time, that says how the home may be marketed (public, private with the signed opt-out, or coming-soon first), what fraud alerts were offered, and who chose what — stored, timestamped, and ready to hand over for three-plus years. No MLS licence is needed to build it; it runs on forms brokers already collect. It completes the paper-trail set this briefing has been building: the exposure receipt (#38), the steering-evidence log (#39), the approval log (#40), the promise log (#41), and now the consent log for the listing itself. Honest limit: a packet proves a choice was documented; it does not prove the conversation behind it was a good one.

Permission watch

  • Catch-up — New York makes public marketing the default for listings (signed Nov 20, reported Nov 23; missed by briefings #38–#41, flagged as a catch-up). Governor Kathy Hochul signed the Fair and Transparent Real Estate Listing Act (Senate Bill S10274). The law requires residential sale and rental listings to be publicly advertised on a platform anyone can see without paying or working with a particular brokerage, unless the seller or landlord signs a standard state disclosure form explaining the risks of limited marketing — including that fewer buyers may see the home and the price may suffer. The signed form must be kept for at least three years. Breaking the law can bring a fine of up to $5,000 and suspension or revocation of a licence. It takes effect in late May 2027 (May 19, 2027 on the 180-day count in legal coverage). New York joins Washington, Connecticut, and Wisconsin in legislating on private listings. For the pre-market aggregator idea in this record (#28, #41): in New York, a private listing is now a documented exception with a signed form attached, not a default — which makes the cooperation/marketing status on each pre-market listing a piece of data with a paper trail behind it. Sources: Real Estate News, Nov 23, https://www.realestatenews.com/2026/11/23/affordability-act-advances-ny-private-listings-bill-becomes-law and HousingWire, https://www.housingwire.com/articles/new-york-private-listing-law/
