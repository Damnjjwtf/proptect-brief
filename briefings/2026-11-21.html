1. NAR publishes its AI rulebook for agents: 11 risks, and "you stay responsible for what the AI does"

Nov 20, Real Estate News (trade coverage of NAR's own resources, promoted at NAR NXT) | https://www.realestatenews.com/2026/11/20/new-resources-tie-nars-code-of-ethics-to-ai-use-transactions

  • What it is. The National Association of Realtors (NAR — the trade group whose ethics code its 1.4 million members must follow) is distributing two new resources built by its Professional Standards Committee. The first, "AI and the Realtor Code of Ethics: 11 Risks Every Realtor Should Know," walks through 11 places where using AI can break the ethics code — false or misleading information, confidential client information, AI-written contract language that is convincing but wrong — and pairs each risk with the code article it touches and a safeguard. The through-line, in the document's own words: the technology is helpful, but "the Realtor remains responsible for how AI is used and for the resulting conduct," and "AI does not expand your license, competence, or authority to practice law." NAR calls it a living document that will grow. The second resource, "The Code of Ethics is Good Business," turns the code into step-by-step checklists for buyer agents and listing agents, in the order a deal actually happens.
  • What it means for product opportunity. This is the licensee-in-the-loop idea (briefings #29, #35) with NAR's own name on it now — not a startup's pitch, the association's official position, handed to every member. That makes the 11 risks a ready-made product spec: an agent tool that checks its own output against those 11 risks before the agent sends anything — contract language, listing photos, client details — and shows the check is answering a document the agent's own association wrote. For Jessica's pitch, the framing sharpens again: a governed tool is no longer "safer than the others," it is the shape NAR is now teaching agents to expect.

2. Zillow puts its Preferred agents on off-market home pages — the fight for sellers moves before the listing exists

Nov 19, Inman Tech Roundup (trade coverage of Zillow's own announcement) | https://www.inman.com/2026/11/19/tech-roundup-zillow-preferred-agents/

  • What it is. Zillow rolled out four consumer tools this week. The one that matters for agents: on the pages for homes that are not for sale, Zillow now shows a short list of its Preferred agent partners — agents in its paid partner program — chosen because they recently sold similar homes nearby, so an owner browsing their own home's value can compare and contact an agent before they have decided to sell. The other three tools are buyer- and renter-side: its BuyAbility affordability tool now pulls a buyer's Experian credit data so the target price updates monthly; new Regional Insights pages describe neighborhoods; and a compare view puts shortlisted homes side by side. (The same roundup's Canva/Constellation item was already reported in briefing #35 and is not repeated here.)
  • What it means for product opportunity. Zillow is buying the moment before the listing — the same pre-market ground this record has tracked from the other side (Zillow Preview in RealScout, #28; Wispr's pre-market marketplace, Demo Day Dec 1). The difference: Preview shares pre-market homes with agents; this tool routes pre-market sellers to Zillow's own paying partners and keeps everyone else off the page. The opening it leaves is the one Inman's own takeaway names — the agent's past-client and neighborhood relationships are the only channel Zillow cannot put a partner list on top of. A tool that works that channel (past-client check-ins timed to real life events, not drip spam) is competing where the portal structurally cannot follow.

3. Two mergers in one day: the map of MLSs and associations keeps shrinking

Updated Nov 20, Real Estate News (trade coverage of the organizations' own announcements) | https://www.realestatenews.com/2026/11/19/georgia-mlss-merge-to-become-better-positioned-for-the-future

  • What it is. Mid Georgia MLS announced Thursday it will merge into First MLS (FMLS) of Atlanta, which already serves more than 55,000 brokers, agents, and appraisers (FMLS's figure, from its release); the two had shared listing data for six years, and FMLS has signed two other data-sharing deals this year. The same day, Nov 20, Denver's two Realtor associations — Denver Metro and South Metro Denver — voted to merge into a single association, Greater Denver Realtors, starting Jan 1, 2027, with roughly 9,500 members combined; the back-office integration runs through 2029. A third item in the same report is a caution flag, not a merger: Edmonton's association vote on joining the Canadian national association directly was adjourned after technical problems with the voting platform and worries about the security of its remote feed.
  • What it means for product opportunity. Every merger is one less gate and one bigger one. For a vendor, fewer MLSs means fewer separate credentialing fights to win national coverage — and each win covers more agents. For Jessica's path specifically, the direction of travel helps the pitch: the industry is consolidating toward larger, better-staffed MLSs that can actually evaluate an AI vendor proposal, instead of hundreds of small ones that stall by default. The Edmonton item is the small print worth remembering: even the vote about the gate now runs on software, and when that software wobbles, the decision stops.

4. VOOV sells agents a person plus AI, not another app

Nov 20, EIN Presswire release (vendor claim — launch release) | https://uspolitics.einnews.com/pr_news/958072637/voov-introduces-ai-powered-real-estate-virtual-assistants-to-help-agents-and-brokerages-scale-smarter

  • What it is. VOOV (Virginia Beach, founded 2021, born inside a brokerage) announced its "All-in-One Real Estate Virtual Assistant": one trained remote staff member per client, working from a supervised office, covering content, admin, marketing, research, and lead follow-up, with AI tools and AI training built into how that person works. The company says it has served more than 200 clients, holds more than 100 five-star Google reviews, and typically cuts a client's operating costs by 30% to 60% versus hiring locally — all company figures, not independently checked. No pricing was disclosed.
  • What it means for product opportunity. A small item, but it confirms a shape at the cheap end of the market: BrokerBot's pitch is "staff you hire, not software you buy" (#36); VOOV is literally selling staff, with AI as the staffer's toolkit rather than the product. For a solo agent — the buyer with no time to learn another dashboard — a supervised human who already knows the tools can beat a better tool. The reverse-engineering note for anything JJ and Jessica build: the competitor for a small agent product is increasingly not another app, it is a person-plus-AI bundle priced like a part-time hire. Whatever ships has to win on the jobs that bundle does badly — the ones needing licensed MLS data and a record of what was decided, which a remote assistant cannot supply.

Whitespace

Build for the day MLSs stop letting vendors copy their data. The catch-up in Permission watch below (NorthstarMLS's CEO, Nov 16) names the direction plainly: vendors today copy MLS data into their own systems and build on the copy; the MLS side wants to flip that — the vendor connects, asks, and gets an answer, but never holds a copy, and the MLS hands the vendor a service promise instead of the other way round. Solid Earth's Recursive (data stays in the MLS's own cloud account, Oct 20) and Cotality's Trestle MCP (approved AI apps query live data under existing licenses) are early builds of exactly that shape. Nobody in this record sells the small vendor's version of it: a thin "ask, don't store" layer — query in place, return the answer with its source and the license it was answered under, keep an audit log, hold nothing. It is the architecture a cautious MLS can say yes to, it survives the copyright and licensing fights because there is no copy to fight over, and it is the natural chassis for the offer-outcome product: the offers were never yours to copy either — you were always going to have to be trusted inside the room.

Permission watch

  • NAR's AI ethics resources (finding 1) are the watch item that moved: guidance, not a rule change — no new prohibition, but the association has now put "the Realtor remains responsible" and 11 named risks in writing for 1.4 million members. Expect MLS and brokerage AI policies to start citing it.
  • Catch-up — "stop copying our data" (Nov 16, Real Estate News; missed by briefings #32–#36, flagged as a catch-up): at the T3 Sixty Edge Tech and Data Forum, NorthstarMLS CEO Tim Dain said MLSs need to "pivot to non-replication" — vendors should access listing data in place rather than store copies, with the MLS or brokerage as the infrastructure and the MLS giving the vendor the service-level promise. CMLS CEO Jessie Edgerton made the companion point on private listings: holding data back to stand out "scoops turf out of the field." Forum talk, not a policy — but it is an MLS CEO naming the architecture the permission layer is moving toward. Source: https://www.realestatenews.com/2026/11/16/how-real-estate-can-avoid-a-pink-boot-data-problem
  • Brokerage "junk fee" suits spread (Nov 20, Real Estate News): a first-time buyer sued Howard Hanna in Pennsylvania state court over a flat $395 "Realtor Transaction Fee" charged on top of commission — the complaint (filed Nov 10, reported Nov 20) alleges the fee was for no service, was labeled and placed on the closing statement to obscure it, and seeks class-action status. The same two law firms sued a Keller Williams brokerage over the same practice last month; the complaint names similar flat fees at Compass ($500) and other Keller Williams offices ($395–$650) as an industry pattern. Allegations, not findings; Howard Hanna says its practices comply with the law. The product read is the FTC Progress Residential lesson (#23) in brokerage form: a fee with no name that describes a service is a liability — and a closing-cost display that names every fee and what it buys is the defensive tool.
