1. HomeStack plugs the branded agent app into ChatGPT and Claude — the MCP wave reaches the buyer's phone

Nov 11, EIN Presswire (vendor release — vendor claim) | https://www.einpresswire.com/article/955914059

  • What it is. HomeStack — the company behind white-label, MLS-connected mobile apps for agents, teams, and brokerages (1,000+ clients, "hundreds of thousands of agents" per the release) — launched HomeStack MCP. It connects an agent's branded app to ChatGPT, Claude, Grok, and other AI assistants through MCP (Model Context Protocol, the open standard that lets an AI assistant securely reach into another platform). An agent can now ask, in the chat tool they already use, "which of my clients have been most engaged this week?" and get an answer drawn from real activity in their branded app — homes viewed, properties saved, neighborhoods searched late at night. The assistant can then build a saved search, assemble a collection of matching listings with notes on why each fits, share it through HomeStack's existing workflow, and suggest push notifications (a price-drop alert for buyers who saved homes in a neighborhood, an open-house reminder for watchers) — with the agent's review and confirmation where required. It also reaches across the stack: one conversation can pull a lead from the CRM (the release names Follow Up Boss), set the person up in the branded app with a tailored search, and draft the follow-up email.
  • What it means for product opportunity. This is the fourth MCP surface in this record — after FBS's Flexmls MCP server (spring), BoldTrail's CRM plug (Oct 14), and Rechat's MCP server (Aug) — and the first one that reaches the consumer-facing app rather than the agent's back office. The pattern is now complete on both sides: the MLS data comes in through one MCP door, the client-activity data comes in through another. Two things to steal: first, the buyer-signal layer from #23 (buyer clicks as a pricing signal) now has an MCP shape — engagement data that any assistant can query, not a dashboard an agent has to open. Second, the "AI does the work, agent approves" line is now the standard pitch in every release — HomeStack says it explicitly for pushes. For Jessica: the offer product's MCP surface should expose the same two things (engagement + offer status) so any assistant can ask "which of my buyers is closest to an offer?" the way HomeStack's asks about engagement. Vendor claims only — no independent check, no customer count beyond the company's own figures, and the "40% higher engagement" figure for its Super Listings video tool is the company's own number.

2. Two MLSs share data across state lines — and a Houston platform makes its first move out of Texas

Nov 12, HousingWire MLS roundup (editor-reviewed; the piece notes it was generated with HousingWire Automation) | https://www.housingwire.com/articles/mls-tech-ai-fraud-partnerships/

  • What it is. Stellar MLS (Florida) and MIBOR Broker Listing Cooperative (Indiana) announced a data-sharing agreement through RPR View — RPR is Realtors Property Resource, NAR's own data platform. Eligible subscribers in each MLS get access to approved off-market listings and historical property data from the other market, aimed at agents serving relocating homeowners, seasonal residents, and buyers with interests in both states. In the same roundup: HAR.com (Houston Association of Realtors) is taking its Platinum technology platform — a subscription suite of ~60 marketing and productivity tools, used by 20,000+ HAR subscribers — outside the Houston area for the first time, through a partnership with LERA MLS in San Antonio.
  • What it means for product opportunity. The rest of the roundup is recap (Big Sky × Property Shield, Flexmls Scout, LERA × RealReports, EquityProtect × CAR — all already in this record). The two new bits both point the same way: MLSs are quietly building the pipes between each other, and the pipes run through platforms the MLSs already trust (RPR, HAR.com) rather than through a new national system. For Jessica: the NorCal MLS Alliance shortcut in the open questions is the same shape — a regional sharing agreement, not a national one — and Stellar/MIBOR shows the template travels: two MLSs, one shared platform, off-market + historical data included. The HAR move is the other half: an MLS's own tech platform becoming a product other MLSs buy. Watch whether RPR View becomes the default pipe for the next few of these agreements, and whether any of them includes the offer-status data an offer product would need — none of them does yet.

3. A Scottsdale broker built his own AI platform instead of buying one — and claims it lifted production

~Nov 8, HousingWire profile (broker's own claims, via a Magic Hand summary of the HousingWire piece) | https://www.themagichand.io/use-cases/broker-frank-vazquez-in-house-ai-incyte-realty

  • What it is. Frank Vazquez, broker-owner of Incyte Realty in Scottsdale, Arizona, built his own tools instead of buying them: a platform called Console (agent-specific CRM environments, AI home search, marketing, listing presentations, CMA generation, automated buyer journeys) plus an AI assistant called Morgan that handles front-desk reception, property sign calls, text conversations, and appointment scheduling. Vazquez told HousingWire average production rose from 6.3 transactions per agent to nearly eight; the brokerage now has 100+ agents, completes about 600 transactions a year, and saw 20% year-over-year volume growth. He says some capabilities are still in development.
  • What it means for product opportunity. This is the build-it-yourself counterpoint to the buy wave that has run all month (Jefe, RealtyMark, BWCONNECT, Maestro). A broker with a software background built the whole stack in-house and is willing to put a production number on it — 6.3 to ~8 deals per agent — which is exactly the "prove production lift, not just save time" test from #29's Leo Pro item. Treat the number as the broker's claim, not a verified result: no independent check, no time period given, and the brokerage is small enough (100+ agents) that a few strong agents could move the average. The reverse-engineering read is the interesting part: the build list (CRM + search + marketing + presentations + CMAs + buyer journeys + a front-desk voice/text assistant) is a complete spec for the solo-to-small-brokerage AI platform, built by one person with a software background. If a single broker can build it, a small team can build a better version and sell it to the brokers who can't.

4. Douglas Elliman goes all-in on AI — with named hires, not just a press release

Nov 10, RISMedia | https://ace.rismedia.com/2026/11/10/douglas-elliman-goes-all-in-for-ai-after-q3s-flat-earnings/

  • What it is. After a flat third quarter (revenue $252.1M, down from $262.8M a year earlier), Douglas Elliman CEO Michael Liebowitz told investors the brokerage will "modernize" and build a "more efficient platform" by early 2027, with technology "central to our strategy." The org chart moved with the words: Sharon Milz hired as head of AI technology transformation, Nick Patino as director of AI enablement, and Jamie Gagliano as chief operations officer. The company says its AI transformation should gradually drive significant savings in non-commission operating expenses over the next three years. The stock jumped 12% on the earnings report, though it is still down 30% this year.
  • What it means for product opportunity. The brokerage-as-buyer thread now has org-chart evidence at a luxury brand: the buyer is not just licensing a tool, it is hiring the people to run the transformation. For a vendor, the door is now a named person (head of AI transformation) rather than a generic "innovation" inbox — and the pitch that lands is cost: non-commission operating expense is the number Elliman's CEO put on the table. Small item; the modernize-by-early-2027 date is a deadline to watch, not a launch.

Whitespace

The cross-vendor approval queue. Every AI tool in this record now does work and asks the agent to approve it — HomeStack's pushes ("once the agent approves"), Rechat's CRM writes, the ad tools in GenLens #31's Agent Inbox item (agents launched nearly 3 in 10 Meta ads on one platform in October, with a human approving first). Each vendor builds its own approval gate, inside its own product. Nobody has built the single queue where an agent reviews everything their tools propose — pushes, follow-ups, ad launches, listing edits — in one place, with the licensee's name on it. That queue is also the compliance artifact the ARA's licensee-in-the-loop campaign will demand: a named licensee, a review step, an audit trail, across vendors instead of inside one. Build the queue, not another assistant — and sell it as the component every other agent tool plugs into.
